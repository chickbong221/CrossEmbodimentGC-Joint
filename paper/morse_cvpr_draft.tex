\begin{abstract}
Training a single policy on offline data from many robots is a promising route toward general-purpose robot control.
Yet as the share of suboptimal data and the diversity of robots grow, the gradients of different robots increasingly
conflict and hinder learning. Conventional approaches mitigate these conflicts at the level of whole robots, while every
joint of every robot is still computed by the same parameters. To address this issue, we propose Morphology-aware
Routing of Shared Experts (MORSE), a novel framework that learns experts at the joint level: every joint selects a few
experts from pools shared across robots, so that two joints share computation only through the experts they both select.
MORSE routes every joint in both the whole-body core and the action decoder by its morphology and state, giving each
joint its own view of the body, while an embodiment-balanced mutual-information objective keeps the routing consistent
and all experts in use. We conduct a comprehensive empirical study on a cross-embodiment offline reinforcement learning
benchmark, demonstrating that MORSE consistently outperforms grouping, gradient-surgery, and mixture-of-experts
baselines, [X].
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =====================================================================================================================

Learning a single policy that controls many different robots has long been a goal of robot
learning~\citep{huang2020smp,bohlinger2024onepolicy}. Recent work pursues it with large cross-embodiment datasets and
models~\citep{oxe2024,octo2024} and with morphology-agnostic policies, which process a variable number of joints and
limbs with shared modules~\citep{gupta2022metamorph,bohlinger2024onepolicy}, while offline reinforcement learning
(RL)~\citep{levine2020offline,kostrikov2022iql} lets such policies learn from logged experience of mixed quality.
Despite these advances, pooling experience across embodiments remains challenging. \citet{abe2026crossembodimentoffline}
found that, as the share of suboptimal data and the number of robot types grow, the policy gradients of different robots
increasingly conflict and hinder learning, most severely for robots with few similar embodiments in the data, such as
bipeds trained alongside many quadrupeds.

Existing remedies treat these conflicts at the level of whole robots: gradient surgery projects the conflicting
gradients of different robots~\citep{yu2020pcgrad}, and Embodiment Grouping updates the policy one group of
morphologically similar robots at a time~\citep{abe2026crossembodimentoffline}. However, in a morphology-agnostic
policy, the gradient of a robot is a sum of contributions from its individual joints, which all update the same
parameters. The conflict between two robots thus mixes joints that may disagree, such as the hip of a quadruped and the
ankle of a biped, with joints that may agree, such as their knees, and the joints of a single robot interact in the same
way. A more desirable approach would let joints whose updates conflict use different parameters, while joints whose
updates agree keep sharing them.

Sparse mixtures of experts (MoE), in which every input activates only a few experts of a large network, have
transformed the scaling of language and vision
models~\citep{shazeer2017outrageously,fedus2022switch,riquelme2021vmoe}, and experts shared across tasks yet selected
per task let related tasks cooperate while unrelated tasks specialize~\citep{ma2018mmoe,chen2023modsquad}. Motivated by
their success, we introduce MORSE (\emph{Morphology-aware Routing of Shared Experts}), a cross-embodiment policy that
learns experts at the joint level. MORSE replaces the whole-body core and the action decoder of a morphology-agnostic
policy~\citep{bohlinger2024onepolicy} by pools of experts shared across robots, and routes every joint to a few experts
of each pool according to its morphology and state. Each joint thus composes its own view of the whole body from the core
experts and its own action from the decoder experts. Because the routing is sparse, two joints share computation only
through the experts they both select, so that conflicting joints can be separated while corresponding joints of similar
robots share experts and transfer what they learn. A mutual-information objective between joint types and experts,
balanced across embodiments, keeps the routing of every joint consistent and all experts in use.

To the best of our knowledge, MORSE is the first cross-embodiment policy that routes individual joints to sparse
experts shared across robots. In contrast to approaches that mitigate conflicts by modifying or regrouping the updates of
a fully shared network, MORSE changes which parameters every joint updates, along the entire pathway from state to
action. We evaluate MORSE on the cross-embodiment offline RL benchmark of~\citet{abe2026crossembodimentoffline}, which
pools datasets of varying quality from 16 legged robots, as a drop-in replacement for the core and the decoder of its
policy. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}, MORSE consistently outperforms grouping, gradient-surgery, and
mixture-of-experts baselines across datasets and base learners, [X].
% [X]: keep what the final runs support, e.g. "with the largest gains on the most suboptimal datasets".

In summary, our main contributions are: (1) We propose MORSE, a cross-embodiment policy that learns experts at the joint
level, so that every joint decides which parameters it shares with other joints; (2) We route every joint in both the
whole-body core and the action decoder according to its morphology and state, which gives every joint its own view of
the body and its own path to its action; (3) We propose an embodiment-balanced mutual-information objective between
joint types and experts that keeps the routing consistent and all experts in use; and (4) We conduct a comprehensive
empirical study on the cross-embodiment offline RL benchmark, including an analysis of the experts that joints share
across robots.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}
% =====================================================================================================================

We consider a set $\mathcal{R}$ of robots with different morphologies. Robot $r$ has actuated joints $\mathcal{J}_r$
and feet $\mathcal{F}_r$, and every joint $j$ and foot $f$ has a fixed-length descriptor $d_j$ or $d_f$ derived from the
robot's model, such as the position, rotation axis, and actuation limits of a joint. A state $s$ comprises local
observations $o_j$ and $o_f$ of the joints and feet and general observations $o^{\mathrm{g}}$, such as the velocity of
the base and the command, and an action sets a target for every joint. In this paper, our goal is a single policy
$\pi_{\theta}(a\mid s,m_r)$, conditioned on the descriptors $m_r$ of the robot, that achieves a high return on every
robot in $\mathcal{R}$. Following \citet{abe2026crossembodimentoffline}, it is learned from a pooled offline dataset
$\mathcal{D}=\bigcup_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\mathcal{D}_r$ of mixed quality, with every robot contributing equally to the
policy loss of a base learner:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\mathcal{L}_r(\theta),\qquad
\mathcal{L}_r(\theta)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}|\,b}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_r}\ell_i(\theta),
\label{eq:actor_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{B}_r$ holds $b$ transitions of robot $r$ and $\ell_i$ is the per-sample policy loss of the base learner,
such as behavior cloning, IQL~\citep{kostrikov2022iql}, or TD3+BC~\citep{fujimoto2021minimalist}.

One might adopt a common approach: a morphology-agnostic policy that shares all of its modules across joints and
robots. URMA~\citep{bohlinger2024onepolicy}, the policy of the benchmark~\citep{abe2026crossembodimentoffline}, encodes
the local observation of every joint and foot with its descriptor and aggregates them, together with $o^{\mathrm{g}}$,
into a vector $x$ of fixed size; a whole-body core $C$ and a decoder $D$ shared by all joints then produce the action
mean $\mu_j$ of every joint:
\begin{equation}
x=A(s,m_r),\qquad z=C(x),\qquad \mu_j=D(z,c_j),
\label{eq:urma}
\end{equation}
where $A$ encodes and aggregates the observations, and $c_j$ holds the local encoding $z^{\mathrm{loc}}_j$ of joint $j$
and an embedding of its descriptor. Every joint of every robot thus reads the same representation $z$ and is decoded by
the same network $D$.

However, full sharing couples every joint to every other. For parameters $\phi$ of the core or the decoder, the gradient
of robot $r$ is a sum of contributions $g_u=(\partial\ell_i/\partial\mu_j)\,\nabla_{\phi}\mu_j$ that flow through the
actions of its joints, $g_r=\nabla_{\phi}\mathcal{L}_r=\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_r}g_u$, where
$\mathcal{U}_r=\mathcal{B}_r\times\mathcal{J}_r$ pairs every transition $i$ of robot $r$ with every joint $j$, and $g_u$
absorbs the normalization of Eq.~\eqref{eq:actor_loss}. A gradient step of size $\alpha$ on the loss of robot $r'$
thus changes the loss of robot $r$, to first order, by
\begin{equation}
\Delta\mathcal{L}_r\approx-\alpha\,\bigl\langle g_r,g_{r'}\bigr\rangle
=-\alpha\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}_r}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}_{r'}}\bigl\langle g_u,g_v\bigr\rangle .
\label{eq:conflict}
\end{equation}
\citet{abe2026crossembodimentoffline} identify negative values of $\langle g_r,g_{r'}\rangle$, i.e., conflicting
gradients between robots, as a main cause of negative transfer in cross-embodiment offline RL. Eq.~\eqref{eq:conflict}
shows that every such conflict is a sum over pairs of joints that all update the same parameters $\phi$, so remedies that
modify or regroup the gradients of whole robots act on these sums as a whole and cannot separate the pairs of joints that
conflict from those that cooperate. This problem becomes even more prominent when few robots share a morphology, since
their joints are then outweighed by those of the majority.

In an effort to address this, we make the sharing of parameters a decision of every joint. We start by reviewing
existing and related work in the next section, and then dive into the proposed scheme.

% =====================================================================================================================
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% =====================================================================================================================

\paragraph{Cross-embodiment policies.}
Morphology-agnostic policies control robots with different numbers of joints and limbs by sharing their modules across
limbs, using graph networks~\citep{wang2018nervenet,huang2020smp},
transformers~\citep{kurin2021amorpheus,gupta2022metamorph}, or attention over joint descriptors, as in
URMA~\citep{bohlinger2024onepolicy}. We build on URMA because the cross-embodiment offline RL
benchmark~\citep{abe2026crossembodimentoffline} adopts it, which lets us compare MORSE directly with the established
baselines. To give limbs different computation, ModuMorph~\citep{xiong2023modumorph} generates
the parameters of the input and output layers of every limb from its morphology with a hypernetwork. Since all limbs
share the hypernetwork, their updates still meet in its parameters, and the generated parameters do not depend on the
state. MORSE instead lets two joints share expert parameters only when they select the same experts, adapts this
selection to the state, and extends it from the action decoder to the whole-body representation.

\paragraph{Gradient conflicts across tasks and embodiments.}
Multi-task optimizers project or reweight conflicting task
gradients~\citep{yu2020pcgrad,liu2021cagrad,navon2022nashmtl} or choose which tasks share an
update~\citep{standley2020which,fifty2021tag,jeong2025sel}, although such methods rarely improve over a well-tuned sum
of the task losses~\citep{xin2022mto,kurin2022unitary}. Architectural remedies turn the layers in which tasks conflict
into task-specific ones~\citep{shi2023recon} or, as HarmoDT does for multi-task offline RL~\citep{hu2024harmodt}, learn
task-specific masks of a shared network.
\citet{abe2026crossembodimentoffline} showed that conflicting gradients between robots hinder cross-embodiment offline RL
and introduced Embodiment Grouping (EG) to mitigate them. These methods act on whole tasks or robots; MORSE addresses
conflicts at the level of joints and conditions the sharing of parameters on the morphology of every joint rather than
on a task identity.

\paragraph{Mixtures of experts.}
Sparse expert layers increase the capacity of a network at a constant cost per
input~\citep{shazeer2017outrageously,lepikhin2021gshard,fedus2022switch,riquelme2021vmoe}. Multi-task models select
shared experts per task~\citep{ma2018mmoe,chen2023modsquad}, and Mod-Squad maximizes the mutual information between
tasks and experts so that every task relies on a few experts and all experts are used~\citep{chen2023modsquad}. In RL,
modules and experts are composed per task~\citep{yang2020softmodularization,sodhani2021care,sun2022paco,hendawy2024moore}
or selected from the state of a single robot across terrains and gaits~\citep{huang2025moeloco}, and in vision-language
models, STGC reroutes tokens whose gradients conflict within their expert~\citep{yang2025stgc}. MORSE makes the joint
the unit of routing across embodiments, conditions the routing on morphology so that corresponding joints of different
robots can share experts, and balances the mutual information between joint types and experts across embodiments.

\section{Morphology-aware Routing of Shared Experts}
\label{sec:method}
% =====================================================================================================================

In this section, we propose MORSE, the framework that underlies our work. Building on sparse mixtures of
experts~\citep{shazeer2017outrageously,fedus2022switch}, we first show that sparse routing confines the pairwise
interaction of Eq.~\eqref{eq:conflict} to the experts that two joints share (Sec.~\ref{sec:locality}). We then turn the
core and the decoder of a morphology-agnostic policy into experts selected per joint (Sec.~\ref{sec:experts}), route
every joint from its morphology and state (Sec.~\ref{sec:routing}), balance the routing with a mutual-information
objective (Sec.~\ref{sec:mi}), and describe training and inference (Sec.~\ref{sec:training}).
Figure~\ref{fig:overview} gives an overview.

\subsection{Sparse Experts Localize Interaction}
\label{sec:locality}

A mixture-of-experts block replaces a module by $K$ experts $E_e(\cdot\,;\phi_e)$ with separate parameters and a router
that assigns every input $u$, here a joint in a transition, logits $\eta_u\in\mathbb{R}^{K}$. Sparse routing keeps the
$k$ largest logits, $\mathcal{S}_u=\operatorname{TopK}(\eta_u,k)$, and renormalizes over them:
\begin{equation}
h_u=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{S}_u}w_{u,e}\,E_e(x_u;\phi_e),\qquad
w_{u,e}=\frac{\exp(\eta_{u,e})}{\sum_{e'\in\mathcal{S}_u}\exp(\eta_{u,e'})}.
\label{eq:sparse_moe}
\end{equation}
The contribution of $u=(i,j)$ to the gradient of expert $e$ is $w_{u,e}\,\hat g_{u,e}$ for $e\in\mathcal{S}_u$, with
$\hat g_{u,e}=(\partial E_e(x_u;\phi_e)/\partial\phi_e)^{\top}\,\partial\ell_i/\partial h_u$, and zero otherwise.
Restricted to the experts of a block, the pairwise term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:conflict} therefore becomes
\begin{equation}
\bigl\langle g_u,g_v\bigr\rangle=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{S}_u\cap\mathcal{S}_v}w_{u,e}\,w_{v,e}\,
\bigl\langle\hat g_{u,e},\hat g_{v,e}\bigr\rangle .
\label{eq:localized}
\end{equation}
Two joints thus interact through the experts only if both select them, in proportion to their routing weights, and
joints with disjoint selections do not interact through the experts of the block at all. A shared module is the special
case $K=k=1$, which couples every pair with weight one, and a dense mixture ($k=K$) couples every pair through every
expert. Sparse routing therefore turns the sharing of computation into a learnable decision for every pair of joints.

\subsection{Joint-Level Experts in the Core and the Decoder}
\label{sec:experts}

\paragraph{Core.}
URMA maps the whole-body input $x$ to a single representation $z$ that every joint reads (Eq.~\eqref{eq:urma}). MORSE
replaces the core by $K$ experts $C_e(\cdot\,;\phi^{\mathrm{C}}_e)$ and lets every joint mix its own subset:
\begin{equation}
z_j=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{C}}_j}w^{\mathrm{C}}_{j,e}\,C_e\bigl(x;\phi^{\mathrm{C}}_e\bigr),
\label{eq:core}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{C}}_j$ and $w^{\mathrm{C}}_{j,e}$ are the experts and weights selected by joint $j$
(Sec.~\ref{sec:routing}). The experts depend only on the transition, so each is evaluated once and reused by every
joint that selects it, while $z_j$ is a joint-specific view of the whole body: a hip and an ankle, or the knees of a
quadruped and of a biped, read the features of the state that matter to them.

\paragraph{Decoder.}
MORSE replaces the shared decoder by $K$ experts $D_e(\cdot\,;\phi^{\mathrm{D}}_e)$ and routes every joint again:
\begin{equation}
\mu_j=\sum_{e\in\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{D}}_j}w^{\mathrm{D}}_{j,e}\,D_e\bigl(z_j,c_j;\phi^{\mathrm{D}}_e\bigr),
\label{eq:decoder}
\end{equation}
where $c_j$ holds the joint's own features, as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:urma}.

Together, Eqs.~\eqref{eq:core} and~\eqref{eq:decoder} give every joint its own sparse path from state to action. By
Eq.~\eqref{eq:localized}, the experts of the core and the decoder couple two joints only if both select them. Joints
that select disjoint experts do not interact through the experts of either block, whereas joints that select the same
experts, such as corresponding joints of similar robots, share them fully, so the policy separates or shares computation
joint by joint rather than robot by robot.

\subsection{Routing from Morphology and State}
\label{sec:routing}

Each block $b\in\{\mathrm{C},\mathrm{D}\}$ has a router that assigns joint $j$ logits from its morphology and the
current state:
\begin{equation}
\eta^{b}_j=G^{b}_{\mathrm{morph}}\bigl(d_j,\bar d_r\bigr)+G^{b}_{\mathrm{state}}\bigl(s^{b}_j\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{K}.
\label{eq:router}
\end{equation}
The morphology branch reads the joint descriptor $d_j$ together with a pooled descriptor $\bar d_r$ of its robot. The
state features are the whole-body input together with the joint's local observation at the core, $s^{\mathrm{C}}_j=
[x,o_j]$, and the joint's representation together with its local encoding at the decoder, $s^{\mathrm{D}}_j=
[z_j,z^{\mathrm{loc}}_j]$. Each branch is a two-layer perceptron with an ELU hidden layer, applied to symlog-normalized
state features. Because all descriptors share one format, corresponding joints of different robots, such as the knees of
two quadrupeds, receive similar descriptors. The morphology branch thus sets a prior over experts that corresponding
joints share and that applies unchanged to new robots, and the state branch lets a joint adapt its experts to its
situation, such as stance and swing. Every joint keeps exactly its $k$ largest logits,
$\mathcal{S}^{b}_j=\operatorname{TopK}(\eta^{b}_j,k)$, with weights $w^{b}_{j,e}$ renormalized as in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:sparse_moe}. The output layers of both branches start from small random weights, so that different joints
begin with different expert sets. The task loss trains the selected experts and, through the routing weights, the
routers.

\subsection{Embodiment-Balanced Mutual Information}
\label{sec:mi}

For experts to specialize, every joint should rely on the same few experts across states, and all experts should be
used. Following Mod-Squad~\citep{chen2023modsquad}, we encode both requirements as the mutual information between
joints and experts. Let a joint type $t=(r,j)$ denote joint $j$ of robot $r$, and let $p^{b}_{i,t}=\operatorname{softmax}
(\eta^{b}_j)$ be its soft routing in transition $i$. Over the transitions $\mathcal{B}_r$ of its robot in a minibatch,
the routing of joint type $t$ and the marginal routing are
\begin{equation}
q^{b}(\cdot\mid t)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_r|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_r}p^{b}_{i,t},\qquad
q^{b}(\cdot)=\sum_{t}\pi_t\,q^{b}(\cdot\mid t),
\label{eq:routing_marginals}
\end{equation}
with $\pi_t=1/(|\mathcal{R}|\,|\mathcal{J}_r|)$, which weights robots equally, as Eq.~\eqref{eq:actor_loss} does, and
the joints of each robot equally. The objective
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}^{b}_{\mathrm{MI}}=\sum_{t}\pi_t\,H\bigl(q^{b}(\cdot\mid t)\bigr)-H\bigl(q^{b}(\cdot)\bigr)
=-I^{b}(T;E)
\label{eq:mi}
\end{equation}
is minimized when every joint type routes consistently to few experts (low conditional entropy) and all experts are
used (high marginal entropy), which balances expert usage without a separate load-balancing term. Because it uses the
soft routing over all $K$ experts, the objective also guides the logits of experts that a joint does not currently
select, and because it is computed on detached router inputs, it updates only the routers. The robot-equal weights
$\pi_t$ give every embodiment the same say in how experts are allocated, regardless of its number of joints.

\subsection{Training and Inference}
\label{sec:training}

The policy is trained with
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}=\mathcal{L}
+\lambda\bigl(\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{C}}_{\mathrm{MI}}+\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{D}}_{\mathrm{MI}}\bigr),
\label{eq:train}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}$ is the policy loss of the base learner (Eq.~\eqref{eq:actor_loss}). Experts, routers, and shared
modules are trained end to end, and the mutual-information term is evaluated at every actor update on all transitions
of its minibatch. Algorithm~\ref{alg:morse} summarizes one training step. Critics and value functions keep the
architecture of the base learner, following~\citet{abe2026crossembodimentoffline}. At inference, the core experts are
evaluated once per transition, every joint mixes $k$ of them and evaluates $k$ decoder experts, and no auxiliary
computation is required.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{One training step of MORSE}
\label{alg:morse}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Minibatch $\mathcal{B}=\bigcup_{r}\mathcal{B}_{r}$; shared modules, core experts $\{C_e\}$, decoder
experts $\{D_e\}$, routers $\{G^{\mathrm{C}},G^{\mathrm{D}}\}$; weight $\lambda$.
\For{each robot $r$ and transition $i\in\mathcal{B}_{r}$}
  \State Encode and aggregate the observation into $x$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:urma}}
  \State Evaluate the core experts $C_e(x)$, $e=1,\dots,K$
  \For{each joint $j\in\mathcal{J}_{r}$}
    \State Route in the core: $\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{C}}_j$, $w^{\mathrm{C}}_j$; form $z_j$
    \Comment{Eqs.~\eqref{eq:core},~\eqref{eq:router}}
    \State Route in the decoder: $\mathcal{S}^{\mathrm{D}}_j$, $w^{\mathrm{D}}_j$; form $\mu_j$
    \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:decoder}}
  \EndFor
\EndFor
\State Compute the base-learner loss $\mathcal{L}$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:actor_loss}}
\State Compute $q^{b}(\cdot\mid t)$, $q^{b}(\cdot)$, and $\mathcal{L}^{b}_{\mathrm{MI}}$ for $b\in\{\mathrm{C},
\mathrm{D}\}$ \Comment{Eqs.~\eqref{eq:routing_marginals},~\eqref{eq:mi}}
\State Update all parameters with $\nabla\mathcal{L}$ and the routers additionally with
$\lambda\nabla\sum_b\mathcal{L}^{b}_{\mathrm{MI}}$ \Comment{Eq.~\eqref{eq:train}}
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\paragraph{Adapting to new embodiments.}
Because its routers consume physical descriptors and state, MORSE applies to robots outside $\mathcal{R}$ without
architectural change. Given a small dataset of a new robot, \emph{router-only adaptation} freezes the experts and the
shared modules and trains only the routers with $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}$, so that adapting to a new body amounts
to deciding which existing experts its joints use.
