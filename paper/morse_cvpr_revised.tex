\begin{abstract}
Cross-embodiment offline reinforcement learning (RL) trains a single policy on data pooled from many robots, and yet
it remains challenging when the robots differ in morphology. Conventional approaches share all parameters across
robots, which causes gradient conflicts between them. Robot-level grouping instead clusters robots by their whole-body
morphology, although robots can be alike in some joints and different in others. We argue that conflicting updates
should be separated into different experts, and that experts should be shared according to the kinematic and dynamic
properties of each joint rather than the whole body. To this end, we propose Morphology-aware Routing of Shared
Experts (MORSE), a framework that learns where to share or specialize by replacing the shared layers of a
cross-embodiment policy with mixtures of experts. A router assigns each joint or the whole body to a few experts based
on its morphology and state. A mutual-information objective further encourages joints with similar kinematic and
dynamic properties to share experts across robots. We conduct a comprehensive empirical study on 16 legged
robots and six datasets of varying quality, demonstrating that MORSE outperforms robot-level grouping and
conflict-resolution methods across three offline RL algorithms.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =====================================================================================================================

Training a single model on large and diverse data has driven much of the recent progress in machine
learning~\citep{brown2020gpt3,radford2021clip}, and robot learning is following the same
path~\citep{oxe2024,octo2024,black2024pi0}. Cross-embodiment learning pools trajectories from many robots to train one
policy that controls all of them, and offline reinforcement learning (RL) extends the usable data from expert
demonstrations to abundant suboptimal trajectories~\citep{kumar2020cql,kostrikov2022iql,chebotar2023qtransformer}.
Their combination has recently been shown to pre-train a single locomotion policy for many legged
robots~\citep{abe2026crossembodimentoffline}. Despite these advances, cross-embodiment learning remains challenging
when the robots differ in morphology: as the number of robot types and the share of suboptimal data grow, training on
the pooled data improves some robots but degrades others~\citep{abe2026crossembodimentoffline}. These observations
motivate policies that can decide what to share across robots and what to keep specific to each.

Sharing across embodiments has been addressed in various ways. Morphology-agnostic architectures process every joint
with shared modules conditioned on a description of the joint and the
body~\citep{huang2020smp,gupta2022metamorph,bohlinger2024onepolicy}. Methods from multi-task learning instead change
how the robots update this shared network, by projecting or reweighting conflicting
gradients~\citep{yu2020pcgrad,navon2022nashmtl}, by grouping tasks with high affinity~\citep{jeong2025sel}, or, in
Embodiment Grouping (EG), by clustering robots according to their whole-body morphology and updating the policy one
group at a time~\citep{abe2026crossembodimentoffline}. A common thread among these approaches is that sharing is never
decided below the level of a whole robot: every joint of every robot is processed by the same parameters, and any
decision about the update applies to all joints of a robot at once.

Yet, which parts of different robots should share computation remains an open question. Robot bodies are alike in
some parts and different in others. The hip-abduction joints of quadrupeds and the hip-roll joints of humanoids, for
example, rotate about similar axes with similar ranges and relative torque limits, whereas other joints of the same
robots serve different purposes, and even within one robot, the joints of a leg play distinct roles in the gait. For
local processing, a joint is therefore better characterized by its kinematic and dynamic properties than by the robot
it belongs to. At the same time, locomotion is coordinated, and the action of each joint depends on the state of the
whole body. A more desirable approach would share computation according to morphological relationships at the level
where that computation operates: individual joints for local processing, and whole embodiments for body-wide
coordination.

Sparse mixtures of experts (MoE) provide a mechanism for such selective sharing. A router sends each input to a few
experts from a shared pool, so that two inputs share parameters only through the experts they both
select~\citep{shazeer2017outrageously,fedus2022switch,riquelme2021vmoe}. In most MoEs, however, the routed unit is a
token or a task. A cross-embodiment policy raises two questions that these models leave open: which unit should be
routed at each stage of the policy, and which morphological relationships the routing should follow.

To this end, we introduce Morphology-aware Routing of Shared Experts (MORSE), a framework that follows this principle to
learn where to share and where to specialize through sparse expert routing. Each expert layer
combines an always-active shared expert, which retains the computation common to all robots, with a pool of routed
experts shared across robots. In the joint-level layers, which encode the observation and predict the action of each
joint, a router conditioned on the properties and the state of the joint selects a few experts for every joint. In the
embodiment-level layers, which process the aggregated body representation, a router conditioned on the embodiment and
its state selects experts for the whole robot, and the result informs the action of every joint. A mutual-information
objective aligned with these two levels encourages structured specialization around groups of similar joints and
around embodiments. Apart from this objective, MORSE leaves the offline RL algorithm unchanged, so it applies to any
offline RL algorithm with an actor loss and complements methods that change how the policy is updated.

In summary, the following are the three main contributions of this paper: (1) We propose MORSE, a framework that
learns morphology-aware sharing at complementary levels, routing every joint to experts shared across robots for local
encoding and action prediction, and every embodiment to experts for whole-body coordination; (2) We introduce a
morphology-aligned routing objective that relates the joint-level experts to groups of joints with similar kinematic
and dynamic properties and the embodiment-level experts to embodiments, under statistics balanced across embodiments;
and (3) We provide a thorough empirical study on 16 legged robots and six datasets, including an ablation of sharing
granularity, layer design, routing objective, capacity, and robot weighting, and an analysis of expert selection.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% =====================================================================================================================

\paragraph{Cross-embodiment policies.}
Cross-embodiment policies differ in where they place the computation that is specific to each body. Large-scale
policies for manipulation and navigation share a trunk across robots and handle their bodies through a common action
space~\citep{oxe2024,black2024pi0} or through embodiment-specific input and output
modules~\citep{octo2024,doshi2024crossformer,wang2024hpt}, both of which treat each embodiment as a whole.
Morphology-agnostic policies take the opposite extreme and share all modules across joints and limbs, using graph
networks~\citep{wang2018nervenet,pathak2019dgn,huang2020smp},
transformers~\citep{kurin2021amorpheus,gupta2022metamorph,trabucco2022anymorph,sferrazza2024bot}, or attention over
joint descriptors~\citep{bohlinger2024onepolicy,ai2025embodimentscaling}. Between these extremes, hypernetworks
generate limb-specific parameters from the morphology of each limb~\citep{xiong2023modumorph,xiong2024hyperdistill},
and M2oE selects experts for each module of a reconfigurable modular robot~\citep{liu2026m2oe}.
% [X] Verify the "first" claim against M2oE before submission.
To the best of our knowledge, MORSE is the first cross-embodiment policy that learns which computation to share at
both the joint and the embodiment level. In contrast to parameters generated from the morphology alone, its expert
selection also depends on the state, and in contrast to module-wise experts for a modular robot, it routes the joints
of heterogeneous robots and couples this local routing with embodiment-level routing for whole-body coordination.

\paragraph{Parameter sharing in multi-task learning.}
When tasks share a network, their updates can interfere and cause negative transfer. Multi-task optimization methods
reduce this interference by modifying the task gradients~\citep{yu2020pcgrad,liu2021cagrad,navon2022nashmtl,liu2023famo}
or by deciding which tasks are updated together~\citep{standley2020which,fifty2021tag,jeong2025sel}, and EG applies
grouping to robots based on the distance between their morphology graphs~\citep{abe2026crossembodimentoffline}.
Allocation methods instead change which parameters the tasks share, by turning the layers with the most conflicts into
task-specific layers~\citep{shi2023recon} or by learning a parameter mask for each task in multi-task offline
RL~\citep{hu2024harmodt}. In all of these methods, the unit of sharing is a task identified by its index, which in
cross-embodiment learning is a whole robot. MORSE shares parameters between joints and between embodiments, decides
the sharing from morphology descriptors and the state rather than from task indices, and learns it during training
without estimating per-task gradients.

\paragraph{Mixtures of experts.}
Beyond scaling model capacity~\citep{shazeer2017outrageously,lepikhin2021gshard,fedus2022switch,riquelme2021vmoe},
MoE layers have been designed to separate common and specialized knowledge, with shared experts that process every
input so that the routed experts can specialize~\citep{dai2024deepseekmoe}. In multi-task learning, task-conditioned
gates select experts for each task~\citep{ma2018mmoe,liang2022m3vit}, and maximizing the mutual information between
tasks and experts encourages task-specific experts~\citep{chen2023modsquad}. In RL, experts and modules are composed
per task for multi-task manipulation~\citep{yang2020softmodularization,sodhani2021care,sun2022paco,hendawy2024moore},
selected from the state of a single legged robot~\citep{huang2025moeloco}, or used to scale value-based
agents~\citep{obandoceron2024moerl}. In MORSE, the variable that specialization follows is defined by morphology rather
than by task identity: groups of joints with similar kinematic and dynamic properties at the joint-level layers and
embodiments at the embodiment-level layers, with statistics balanced across embodiments so that robots with many
joints do not dominate.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:prelim}
% =====================================================================================================================

Let $\mathcal{R}$ be a set of robots with different morphologies. Robot $r$ has actuated joints $\mathcal{J}_r$, and
every joint $j$ is described by a fixed-length descriptor $d_j$ derived from the robot model, such as its rotation
axis, range, and torque limit; $m_r$ collects the descriptors of robot $r$. A state $s$ comprises a local observation
$o_j$ of every joint and a general observation $o^{\mathrm{g}}$, such as the base velocity and the command, and an
action $a=(a_j)_{j\in\mathcal{J}_r}$ sets a target for every joint. Given a pooled offline dataset
$\mathcal{D}=\bigcup_{r}\mathcal{D}_r$ of mixed quality, our goal is to learn a single policy
$\pi_\theta(a\mid s,m_r)$ that maximizes the return of every robot. An offline RL algorithm trains $\pi_\theta$ by
minimizing an actor loss in which all robots have equal weight~\citep{abe2026crossembodimentoffline},
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}|}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_r|}
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_r}\ell_i(\theta),
\label{eq:actor_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{B}_r$ holds the transitions of robot $r$ in a minibatch and $\ell_i$ is the per-sample loss of the
algorithm, \eg, the advantage-weighted log-likelihood of IQL~\citep{kostrikov2022iql}.

Morphology-agnostic policies handle the varying number of joints by combining per-joint and whole-body
computations~\citep{huang2020smp,gupta2022metamorph,bohlinger2024onepolicy}:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
z^{\mathrm{loc}}_j&=F(o_j),\\
z&=C\bigl(A(\{z^{\mathrm{loc}}_j,d_j\}_{j\in\mathcal{J}_r},o^{\mathrm{g}})\bigr),\\
\mu_j&=D(z,z^{\mathrm{loc}}_j,d_j).
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:policy}
\end{equation}
A joint encoder $F$ encodes every joint, an order-invariant aggregation $A$ forms a fixed-size body representation, a
core $C$ processes it, and a decoder $D$ predicts the action mean $\mu_j$ of every joint. We call $F$ and $D$
joint-level maps and $C$ an embodiment-level map.

Conditioning on $d_j$ lets these maps compute different functions for different joints, but not with different
parameters: every joint updates all parameters of the joint-level maps, and every robot all parameters of the
embodiment-level map, whether their
morphologies are alike or not. The sharing is thus fixed by the architecture rather than learned.

\section{Morphology-aware Routing of Shared Experts}
\label{sec:method}
% =====================================================================================================================

In this section, we present MORSE, which turns the maps of Eq.~\eqref{eq:policy} into expert layers whose experts are
shared across robots (Sec.~\ref{sec:experts}). Its core lies in what each layer routes and what the routing follows
(Sec.~\ref{sec:routing}): joint-level layers route every joint and align its experts with groups of joints that have
similar kinematic and dynamic properties, while embodiment-level layers route the whole robot and align its experts
with its embodiment. Figure~\ref{fig:overview} gives an overview.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\fbox{\rule{0pt}{1.6in}\rule{0.95\textwidth}{0pt}}
\caption{Overview of MORSE. (a) An expert layer combines an always-active shared expert with routed experts selected by
a router that reads the morphology and the state of the routed unit. (b) Joint-level expert layers in the joint
encoder $F$ and the decoder $D$ route every joint and align its experts with its joint group; embodiment-level expert
layers in the core $C$ route every robot and align its experts with its embodiment. The body representation $z$
reaches every joint decoder and its router. All experts and routers are shared across robots.}
\label{fig:overview}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{Expert Layers}
\label{sec:experts}

An expert layer $b$ consists of a shared expert $f^b_0$, which is always active, and $K-1$ routed experts
$f^b_1,\dots,f^b_{K-1}$, of which a router selects $k$ for every routed unit $t$:
\begin{equation}
y=f^b_0(x)+\sum_{e=1}^{K-1}G^e_b(t)\,f^b_e(x),
\label{eq:expert_layer}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
h_b(t)&=\psi^{\mathrm{m}}_b(d_t)+\psi^{\mathrm{s}}_b(s^b_t),\\
G_b(t)&=\operatorname{TopK}\bigl(\operatorname{Softmax}(h_b(t))\bigr),
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:router}
\end{equation}
where $x$ is the layer input of $t$, and the router scores the routed experts from a morphology descriptor $d_t$ and a
state $s^b_t$ of the unit (Sec.~\ref{sec:routing}). The shared expert holds the computation that every robot needs, so
that the routed experts can specialize~\citep{dai2024deepseekmoe}. As is common for sparse expert layers, the kept weights are not renormalized
and learned noise perturbs the scores during training; the details are given in the supplementary material. Because
the routing is sparse, a unit updates only the shared expert and the experts it selects, so that which units share
parameters is no longer fixed by the architecture but learned by the routers.

\subsection{Morphology-Aligned Routing}
\label{sec:routing}

At each level, a routed unit $t$ has a morphology descriptor $d_t$, a state $s^b_t$, and a group $y_t$ that its
routing should follow.

\paragraph{Joint level.}
In the joint encoder $F$ and the decoder $D$, the routed unit is a joint of a transition, $t=(r,j)$, with $d_t=(d_j,\bar d_r)$, where $\bar d_r$
summarizes robot $r$ by its mean joint descriptor and its number of joints. The state is the joint
observation $o_j$ in the encoder and $(z,z^{\mathrm{loc}}_j)$ in the decoder, so that the choice of action expert also
depends on the whole body; every expert of the decoder is a complete action head with its own embedding of $d_j$. The group $y_t$ is the
joint group of $j$. We form the joint groups once, before training, by clustering the joints of all robots with
$k$-means on their kinematic and dynamic properties: rotation axis, number of child joints, nominal position, range,
torque and velocity limits relative to the strongest joint of the robot, damping, armature, stiffness, and friction. The groups therefore cross robot boundaries; for example,
the hip-abduction joints of all quadrupeds share a group with the hip-roll joints of the humanoids (see the
supplementary material).

\paragraph{Embodiment level.}
In the core $C$, the routed unit is the whole robot at a transition, $t=r$, with $d_t=\bar d_r$, the layer input as its state,
and the robot itself as its group. Robots with similar bodies can thus learn to share experts for the body representation,
while dissimilar ones use different experts.

\paragraph{Routing objective.}
The actor loss of Eq.~\eqref{eq:actor_loss} trains the routers, but it does not ask units of the same group to rely
on the same experts. At every expert layer $b$, MORSE therefore maximizes the mutual information between the group of
a unit and its experts. With $p_{b,i}(t)=\operatorname{Softmax}(h_b(t))$ in transition $i$, we estimate how often
unit $t$ uses expert $e$ from the transitions $\mathcal{B}_r$ of its robot, and weight the units so that every robot
counts equally:
\begin{equation}
P_b(e\mid t)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_r|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{B}_r}p^e_{b,i}(t),\quad
P(t)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{R}|\,|\mathcal{J}_r|},
\label{eq:routing_marginals}
\end{equation}
with $P(r)=1/|\mathcal{R}|$ at the embodiment level. Summing $P(t)$ over the units of group $y$ gives $P(y)$, the
$P(t)$-weighted mean of $P_b(e\mid t)$ over these units gives $P_b(e\mid y)$, and $P_b(e)=\sum_yP(y)\,P_b(e\mid y)$.
The mutual information is
\begin{equation}
I_b(Y;E)=H\bigl(P_b(E)\bigr)-\sum_{y}P(y)\,H\bigl(P_b(E\mid y)\bigr),
\label{eq:mi}
\end{equation}
where $H$ is the entropy. The first term rewards the use of all experts, and the second rewards every group for
concentrating on a few. The balanced weights prevent robots with many joints from dominating the statistics, and since
the objective acts on routing probabilities averaged over states, it sets a prior for every group without fixing the
expert of any unit. MORSE adds this objective to the actor loss,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{train}}=\mathcal{L}-\lambda\sum_bI_b(Y;E)+\beta\sum_b\operatorname{KL}\bigl(P_b(E)\,\|\,u\bigr),
\label{eq:train}
\end{equation}
where $u$ is uniform over the routed experts. Since $\operatorname{KL}(P_b(E)\,\|\,u)=\log(K-1)-H(P_b(E))$, the last
term is the first term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mi}, a load-balancing term that acts from the start of training, whereas
$\lambda$ is switched on after a warm-up so that routes first form from the task. The critics and the losses of the
offline RL algorithm are unchanged.

\paragraph{Experimental setup.}
The benchmark pools the data of nine quadrupeds, six bipeds, and a hexapod in six datasets: Expert, Expert Replay, and
70\% Suboptimal, each with a forward and a backward walking command. We combine MORSE with three offline RL
algorithms, BC, TD3+BC~\citep{fujimoto2021minimalist}, and IQL~\citep{kostrikov2022iql}, and compare with these
algorithms alone, with IQL combined with task grouping (SEL)~\citep{jeong2025sel} or gradient surgery
(PCGrad)~\citep{yu2020pcgrad}, and with Embodiment Grouping (EG) on all three, using the results reported
by~\citet{abe2026crossembodimentoffline}. We instantiate MORSE on URMA~\citep{bohlinger2024onepolicy}, the
architecture adopted by the benchmark, so that the comparison with the reported results is direct: a layer of the
joint encoder, every second layer of the core, and the decoder become expert layers, and all other components are
unchanged. Every expert layer has $K=16$ experts, one of them shared, of which $k=3$ routed experts are selected, and
the joints form eight groups. We set $\beta=10^{-2}$ and $\lambda=5\cdot10^{-4}$, which is switched on after 10k
updates and ramped in over 5k. As EG takes one optimizer step for each of its four groups in every update, MORSE takes
four actor steps per update. Every 100 updates, we evaluate one episode per robot and report the mean return over all
16 robots; for MORSE, we report the evaluation with the highest mean return after 50k updates [X: one seed so far].
Architecture details and the remaining hyperparameters are given in the supplementary material.

\paragraph{Expert data.}
On the Expert datasets, MORSE performs on par with the strongest methods [X]. With near-optimal data, all methods
except TD3+BC are within a point of each other, and the choice of what to share leaves little room for improvement.

\paragraph{Suboptimal data.}
As the share of suboptimal trajectories grows, the gain of MORSE increases [X: Expert Replay]. On 70\% Suboptimal
Forward, MORSE raises the return of IQL to 58.1, compared with 51.2 for the best baseline [X: Backward], in the
setting where pooling the data of different robots helps least~\citep{abe2026crossembodimentoffline}.

\paragraph{Across offline RL algorithms.}
MORSE improves BC and TD3+BC by a similar margin [X]. Since it changes only the policy network, its gain does not
depend on the algorithm that trains it.

\paragraph{Per-robot results.}
The gain concentrates on robots with few morphologically similar counterparts (Table~\ref{tab:robots}): MORSE learns
the bipeds NAO and Cassie, on which EG obtains almost no return, and matches EG on the other robots except A1. Neither
method learns the hexapod.

\paragraph{Sharing granularity.}
Grouping robots for the update improves over the dense policy [X], but expert layers at either level improve more,
and only routing at both levels achieves the full gain [X]. Joint-level routing alone lets similar joints share
experts but processes every body with the same core, whereas embodiment-level routing alone separates bodies but keeps
every joint on the same encoder and decoder. The two levels are therefore complementary.

\paragraph{Layer design.}
Removing the shared expert lowers the return [X], since every routed expert must then also learn the computation
common to all robots. Renormalizing the top-$k$ weights lowers it as well [X], since the routed experts then dominate
from the first updates, before the router has learned which units belong together. Removing $z$ from the decoder
router [X] shows that the choice of action expert benefits from the state of the whole body.

\paragraph{Routing objective.}
Grouping joints by their kinematic and dynamic properties outperforms training without the routing objective,
grouping by robot identity, and random groups of the same sizes [X]. Robot identity prevents similar joints of
different robots from sharing experts, and random groups impose structure that does not follow morphology. Weighting
every joint equally instead of every robot lowers the return [X], since robots with many joints then dominate the
routing statistics.

\paragraph{Capacity and robot weighting.}
A dense policy of matched capacity does not close the gap [X], so the gain of MORSE is not explained by the number of
parameters. EG's grouped updates weight every robot by the inverse size of its group; applying these weights to a
dense policy [X] recovers part of the gain of EG but not that of MORSE, and combining MORSE with the grouped updates
[X] shows that the two are complementary.

\paragraph{Sharing across robots.}
At the joint-level layers, every group concentrates on a few experts, and joints of the same group select the same
experts across robots [X]. For example, the hip-abduction joints of the quadrupeds and the hip-roll joints of the
humanoids share their decoder experts (Fig.~\ref{fig:selection}a).

\paragraph{Specialization within a robot.}
Within a robot, joints of different groups select different experts [X], so that sharing across robots does not merge
the joints of one robot onto common computation (Fig.~\ref{fig:selection}b). This specialization is strongest in the
decoder [X], where the actions of different joints differ most.

\paragraph{State dependence.}
A joint changes its experts with its state [X], for example between stance and swing (Fig.~\ref{fig:selection}c),
while its group keeps the same small set of experts, as intended by the routing objective (Sec.~\ref{sec:routing}).

\paragraph{Embodiment-level routing.}
At the embodiment-level layers, robots with similar bodies select the same experts [X], and the resulting clusters [X:
agree with or refine] the morphology-graph groups of EG, although MORSE is given no grouping of robots.

\paragraph{Interaction between robots.}
Since a joint updates only the shared expert and the experts it selects (Sec.~\ref{sec:experts}), robots of different
morphology interact mainly through the shared expert in MORSE [X]. In the dense policy, the gradients of different
robots are mostly aligned in the core [X], which supports holding their common computation in a shared expert.
